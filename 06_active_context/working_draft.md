# Chapter 1 — The Bin

The bin belongs to Daphne before Milo reaches it.

Not by law. Not by teeth. Not tonight.

The alley behind the Golden Carp is wet enough to remember rain, though the sky has cleared and the city has gone hard and black above the roofs. Neon trembles in the puddles. Steam crawls out of a vent near the kitchen door and spreads through the smoke already hanging there, thick with fish, garlic, frying oil, and the sour rot of cabbage gone soft in plastic bags.

Daphne sits beside the main trash bin as if someone placed her there with purpose.

Her paws are close together. Her short blue-gray coat takes the red leaking from the kitchen window and turns it to steel. Her green eyes hold the alley without hurry. Nothing else in her moves.

Milo comes from under the broken crate.

He comes too quickly at first, then remembers himself halfway across the concrete and slows. His tail lifts. Drops. Lifts again. The white patch on his chest is too clean for this place, or too young to know better. He looks at the bin, then at Daphne, then at the strip of darkness beneath the lid where a fish bone has wedged itself like a promise.

Daphne does not blink.

Milo stops.

Above them, from inside the kitchen, metal strikes wood. Once. Twice. A cleaver, heavy and certain. The sound travels through the back door and settles between the cats.

Milo lowers his head. Not enough to surrender. Enough to suggest he has considered it.

Daphne's left ear turns toward him.

That is all.

Old Bao watches from the warm place by the vent. The dog is a large shadow with clouded eyes and scarred ears, his muzzle resting on his paws. He could stand and cross the alley in three heavy movements. He does not. When Daphne arrived earlier, he had opened one eye, judged the matter, and moved his tail out of her way.

Milo knows this. Or he knows the shape of it without understanding the age inside it.

He takes one more step.

Daphne's eyes narrow.

The step becomes a stretch. Milo extends his front paws, arches his back, opens his mouth in a silent yawn too wide to be honest. He is suddenly not approaching the bin at all. He is merely young. Merely flexible. Merely present in the same world as fish bones and old rice.

Daphne lets the lie exist.

For a while, that is the whole contest: the bin, the damp concrete, the unspent distance between them. No growl. No hiss. No rush of bodies. Only Milo's foolish little advances and Daphne's refusal to spend energy proving what the alley already knows.

The kitchen door opens.

Heat spills out first, then light.

Liang Wei stands in the doorway under a tall white cook's hat, his dirty apron tied loose over a sleeveless undershirt and the old cleaver hanging from one hand. The blade is clean at the edge and dark near the handle, as if the years have soaked into it. A cigarette rests unlit behind his ear, tucked low beneath the hat's edge. He looks down at the cats, then at the bin, then at Old Bao pretending not to be awake.

"Aiya. Committee meeting?" he says.

Milo startles backward so fast his rear paw lands in a shallow puddle. Daphne does not move.

Liang laughs through his nose and goes back inside.

The door swings shut.

Inside the Golden Carp kitchen, the air is hotter and meaner. Oil snaps in a wok. Water runs in the sink. Somewhere beyond the narrow pass, Mei-Lin Chen's voice cuts through the clatter with the precision of a receipt being torn in half.

"Liang. Trash after table seven."

"I know, I know. Mafan."

He says it to the cutting board, not to her.

The cleaver comes down through scallion stems, then through the joint of a fish head. Liang's fingers move close to the blade without fear. Smoke from the burners folds into the sweat at his neck. The scrap bowl sits near the back of the counter, half hidden behind a dented pot: skin, rice, a curl of pork fat, something with enough sauce still clinging to it to matter.

Milo appears under the lower shelf.

Liang sees the white chest first.

"No," he says.

Milo freezes.

"That means you."

Milo's ears tilt forward.

Liang points the flat of the cleaver toward the back door. "Out."

Milo looks at the bowl.

The cook looks at the cat. The cat looks at the bowl. The kitchen breathes heat around them both.

Liang mutters something in Chinese under his breath, too low and tired to be a real curse. Then he pinches a small piece of fish between two fingers and drops it near the shelf, not near his shoes, not near the door. Close enough for Milo to take without courage becoming stupidity.

"One," Liang says. "You tell her I gave you one, I deny everything."

Milo darts forward, takes the fish, and retreats with it sideways, as if sideways movement makes him invisible.

Liang snorts. The cleaver rises again.

By the time he comes back into the alley, the trash bag is heavy in one hand and the cigarette is in his mouth. The lighter clicks once, twice, then catches. Flame shows the lines beside his eyes. He draws smoke in, holds it, and lets it out toward the brick wall.

Daphne has moved onto the trash bin lid.

Milo sits below her, not close enough to be corrected, not far enough to be innocent. A silver fish spine lies between his paws. He has not touched it yet.

Old Bao exhales from the vent.

Liang tosses the bag into the second bin and wipes his hand on his apron. The smoke makes a veil between him and the cats. For a moment he looks like part of the alley's weather: grease, tobacco, old patience.

He nods at Daphne first.

She gives him nothing.

Then he looks at Milo and points the cigarette at him, ember bright.

"Still alive, xiao tou?"

Milo lifts his chin. Not proud. Not ashamed. Only young enough to think both things might taste the same.

Liang laughs quietly.

Daphne lowers one paw from the lid to the rim of the bin. The movement is small. Final. Milo looks at the fish spine, then at her, then at the darker mouth of the alley beyond Old Bao's warm vent.

He takes the spine.

Not because he has won.

Because Daphne has allowed the night to continue.
